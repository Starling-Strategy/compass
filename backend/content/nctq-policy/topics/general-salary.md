# General Salary

## Topic Brief

Traditionally, teachers earn higher salaries over time in two main ways: gaining more years of experience and completing additional education, such as earning a master's degree or taking extra professional courses - often referred to as a "step and lane" salary schedule. School districts frequently give teachers automatic raises as they reach these milestones, along with cost-of-living adjustments. While states often determine the basic structure of teacher pay, the specifics of teacher salaries are more often set at the district level.

Pathfinder tracks:

- Pay for preservice and uncertified teachers.
- General salary information such as the structure of the salary schedule and maximum available salary.
- Pay for prior work experience.
- Annual salary for teachers with a bachelor's at different points in their career.
- Annual salary for teachers with a master's at different points in their career.
- Longevity pay.

Additional pay for working in hard-to-staff schools or subjects is covered in the Differentiated Pay topic.

## Stances

### [stance:general-salary-competitive-pay] Competitive Pay

Districts need to pay all teachers like professionals and pay teachers more for working where they're most needed. This means paying them akin to similarly educated professionals and paying more for teachers teaching in hard to staff schools and subjects.

### [stance:general-salary-masters-degree-pay] Master's Degree Pay

Districts should not pay teachers more for having a master's degree. Research has only found positive effects of earning a masters degree for secondary teachers earning a masters in their subject area, specifically in mathematics.

### [stance:general-salary-frontloaded-schedules] Frontloaded Schedules

To support retention in early years when teachers are most likely to leave the profession, districts should shift their salary schedules to frontload teacher pay more, or offer greater annual raises to teachers earlier in their career, rather than granting the largest raises to teachers late in their career.

## Research Rationales

### [rationale:general-salary-competitive-pay] Competitive Pay

- stance_id: stance:general-salary-competitive-pay
- source_id: source:nctq-research-general-salary
- source_title: NCTQ Research
- source_url: https://www.nctq.org/district-policy-pathfinder/research-rationales/general-salary/
- citation_status: ready

Research demonstrates that increased base pay attracts more teachers. A Virginia study found that a $5,000 increase in starting salaries is associated with a 16% decrease in teacher vacancies. Competitive wages relative to other professions help attract and retain quality candidates.

### [rationale:general-salary-masters-degree-pay] Master's Degree Pay

- stance_id: stance:general-salary-masters-degree-pay
- source_id: source:nctq-research-general-salary
- source_title: NCTQ Research
- source_url: https://www.nctq.org/district-policy-pathfinder/research-rationales/general-salary/
- citation_status: ready

Extensive research finds that master's degree premiums show minimal impact on teacher effectiveness. The only consistent exception is secondary math teachers with a subject-area master's. Automatic step-and-lane salary increases for advanced degrees are an inefficient use of limited compensation budgets.

### [rationale:general-salary-frontloaded-schedules] Frontloaded Schedules

- stance_id: stance:general-salary-frontloaded-schedules
- source_id: source:nctq-research-general-salary
- source_title: NCTQ Research
- source_url: https://www.nctq.org/district-policy-pathfinder/research-rationales/general-salary/
- citation_status: ready

Teacher attrition is highest in the first five years. Frontloading salary growth to the early career years helps retain teachers during the critical period when they are developing expertise. Detroit restructured its salary schedule to provide substantial pay increases within the first five years.
